3.2 Matrix Algebra

Section 3.1 left the laboratory hanging. The spectrophotometer measured absorbances \mathbf{A} = (0.78, 0.74) for a two-dye mixture, the absorptivity matrix E is known from calibration, and the concentrations satisfy E\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{A} — an equation whose answer is on the wrong side. In scalar arithmetic the move would be reflexive: divide by E. But E is a grid, and “divide by a grid” means nothing until we build it meaning. The construction route runs through three ideas that are the algebra of matrices: multiplying matrices by each other, an identity that multiplication cannot alter, and — for the matrices that deserve one — an inverse that undoes. By the end of the section the dye concentrations fall out in two lines, and the one number that decides whether they can fall out will be staring at us, waiting for Section 3.4 to give it a name.

Multiplying grids

Why would two matrices multiply? The honest motivation is composition: doing one linear operation after another. Convert feed amounts to monomer totals (B), then monomer totals to element totals (A) — the net conversion from feeds to elements is some single grid, and it ought to be computable from A and B. The requirement that “apply B, then apply A” equal “apply the combined grid” forces the definition: the combined grid’s action on any vector must be A(B\mathbf{v}), and working out what grid does that job yields the rule every textbook states.

Definition 3.2.1 (Matrix product). For A (m \times n) and B (n \times p), the product AB is the m \times p matrix with (AB)_{ij} = \text{(row } i \text{ of } A) \cdot \text{(column } j \text{ of } B) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}. Equivalently, column by column: the j-th column of AB is A applied to the j-th column of B. Inner dimensions must match; the outer ones set the answer’s shape.

The column reading shows the definition doing exactly the promised job — AB’s columns are A’s outputs on B’s columns, so AB acting on anything reproduces apply-B-then-A. Two consequences follow, one comforting and one that must be internalized early. The comforting one: the product is associative, (AB)C = A(BC), because both sides are the single grid performing “apply C, then B, then A” — grouping the paperwork differently cannot change the pipeline (a direct double-sum verification is Exercise 7). The uncomfortable one:

Theorem 3.2.2 (Multiplication does not commute). In general AB \neq BA.

Proof. One counterexample suffices: \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

This is not an algebraic curiosity but a fact about the world that matrices inherit honestly: operations performed in different orders end differently. Rotate a molecule 90° about z and then reflect it in the xz-plane, or reflect first and rotate second — the atom ends in different places (Section 3.3 will compute both). Sequence matters in the lab, and the algebra keeps the books accordingly. Every manipulation below respects it: we will multiply on a chosen side and say which.

The identity, and undoing

Scalar algebra’s 1 has a matrix counterpart: the identity matrix I, with 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere (I\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} and AI = IA = A — the do-nothing operation). With a “1” in hand, “division” can be given its honest form: not dividing, but multiplying by an undoer.

Definition 3.2.3 (Inverse). A square matrix A is invertible if there exists a matrix A^{-1} with A^{-1} A = A A^{-1} = I. Then A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} is solved in one move: multiply both sides on the left by A^{-1}, and associativity gives \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}.

For 2 \times 2 matrices the inverse has a closed form, and deriving it teaches more than memorizing it. Seek A^{-1} for A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}. A guess with swapped diagonal and negated off-diagonal, M = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}, is worth auditioning because multiplying it out collapses beautifully: M A = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} da - bc & db - bd \\ -ca + ac & -cb + ad \end{pmatrix} = (ad - bc)\, I . The off-diagonals cancelled identically, and both diagonal entries came out equal to the same number ad - bc. So M is almost the inverse — it undoes A up to an overall scalar — and dividing by that scalar finishes the job, provided the scalar is not zero.

Theorem 3.2.4 (The 2×2 inverse). If ad - bc \neq 0, \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}, and if ad - bc = 0 no inverse exists.

The quantity ad - bc appeared uninvited, decided everything, and will not go away: it is the determinant, and §3.4 is devoted to what it means. For now, note the grammar of the theorem — one computable number sorts all 2\times2 matrices into the invertible and the lost causes.

Example 3.2.5 (The dye assay, solved). Recover the concentrations from E\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{A} with E = \begin{pmatrix} 15000 & 3000 \\ 2000 & 11000 \end{pmatrix} M⁻¹cm⁻¹ and measured \mathbf{A} = (0.78, 0.74).

Setting it up. Compute ad - bc; if nonzero, apply Theorem 3.2.4 and multiply.

Solution. ad - bc = (15000)(11000) - (3000)(2000) = 1.65 \times 10^8 - 6 \times 10^6 = 1.59 \times 10^8. Then \mathbf{c} = E^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{1.59 \times 10^8} \begin{pmatrix} 11000 & -3000 \\ -2000 & 15000 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.78 \\ 0.74 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{1.59 \times 10^8} \begin{pmatrix} 6360 \\ 9540 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4.0 \times 10^{-5} \\ 6.0 \times 10^{-5} \end{pmatrix} \text{M}.

Check. Round trip: these are exactly the concentrations that §3.1’s forward computation turned into (0.78, 0.74) — inverse and original compose to the identity in the numbers, not just the notation. Units: \mathbf{A} is dimensionless and E^{-1} carries M·cm, giving molar concentrations ✓. Magnitude: 10^{-5} M for dyes with \varepsilon \sim 10^4 giving A \sim 1 is textbook-plausible ✓. The negative entries of E^{-1} are not errors — they are the algebra subtracting away each dye’s spectral intrusion into the other’s wavelength.

Remarks and cautions

Side matters, twice. Because multiplication does not commute, solving A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} requires multiplying both sides on the left: \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}, never \mathbf{b}A^{-1} (which is usually not even defined). Writing \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}/A is not shorthand — it is ambiguous about the side and therefore about the answer.

Inverses reverse order. (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}: to undo “socks, then shoes,” remove shoes first. The proof is one associativity chain — (B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1} I B = I — and the exam error it prevents ((AB)^{-1} = A^{-1}B^{-1}) survives only in a commuting world, which Theorem 3.2.2 closed.

Not every square matrix has an inverse. When ad - bc = 0, Theorem 3.2.4’s construction divides by zero and no repair exists. The failure is chemical, not clerical: for the dye assay, \det E = 0 happens exactly when one dye’s absorptivity column is a multiple of the other’s — spectra of the same shape — and then two wavelengths genuinely cannot tell the dyes apart. No algebra recovers information the measurement never contained; §3.4 turns this into a working design criterion.

AB = AC does not imply B = C. Cancellation needs an inverse: only if A^{-1} exists may you strike A from both sides. With singular A, different inputs can produce identical outputs — the algebraic face of the same information loss.

Summary

Matrix multiplication is composition: (AB)_{ij} is row-i-dot-column-j, engineered precisely so that AB acts as “apply B, then A” — hence associative, and hence not commutative, because sequenced operations in the world are not either. The identity I plays 1, and an invertible matrix has an undoer A^{-1} that solves A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} as \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} in a single left-multiplication; for 2 \times 2 matrices the inverse is explicit, exists exactly when ad - bc \neq 0, and delivered the dye concentrations from measured absorbances with a round-trip check against the forward problem. The residue of the section is that one deciding number: it emerged from the algebra unbidden, it separates solvable assays from hopeless ones, and its meaning — geometric, not algebraic — is the business of the determinant section ahead.

Exercises

  1. For A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} and B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}: compute AB and BA, confirm they differ, and describe in words what right-multiplication by B did to A versus left-multiplication.

  2. Dimension bookkeeping: for P (2 \times 3), Q (3 \times 3), R (3 \times 2), which of PQ, QP, PR, RP, PQR are defined, and with what shapes?

  3. Verify that I earns its name: compute IA and AI for the A of Exercise 1, and I\mathbf{v} for \mathbf{v} = (5, -2).

  4. Invert \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix} and \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} — or explain, via Theorem 3.2.4’s condition, why one of them refuses.

  5. A two-protein assay obeys E\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{A} with E = \begin{pmatrix} 8000 & 4000 \\ 3000 & 9000 \end{pmatrix} M⁻¹cm⁻¹ and measured \mathbf{A} = (0.52, 0.57). Find both concentrations, and run the round-trip check.

  6. Using Exercise 1’s matrices, verify associativity numerically: compute (AB)B and A(BB) and confirm agreement. Why does this NOT follow from commutativity (which is false)?

  7. ★ Prove associativity in general: show \left((AB)C\right)_{ij} = \sum_k \sum_l a_{ik} b_{kl} c_{lj} = \left(A(BC)\right)_{ij}, noting that only the order of two finite summations is exchanged.

  8. ★ Prove the socks-shoes law (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} by verifying both defining products, then produce a 2\times2 numerical counterexample showing A^{-1}B^{-1} fails.

  9. ★ Your two “dyes” have absorptivity columns (10000, 4000) and (5000, 2000) M⁻¹cm⁻¹. Show the assay matrix is singular, explain spectroscopically what property of the two dyes doomed it, and propose the experimental fix (what must be true of a third wavelength for a 2\times2 sub-assay built from it to work?).

  10. ★★ Matrix powers: for the “relaxation” matrix T = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} (columns: where the population of each of two conformers goes in one time step — note each column sums to 1), compute T^2 and T^4 \mathbf{p}_0 for \mathbf{p}_0 = (1, 0), and conjecture the population vector as the number of steps grows. (You are watching a Markov chain approach equilibrium; Chapter 6 will catch the limit exactly, and the tool that does it — eigenvalues — lives one chapter past the determinant.)
